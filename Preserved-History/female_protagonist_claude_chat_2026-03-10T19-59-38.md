could women be scribes in ancient babylonia, Assyria, or Mesopotamia?

Yes. Women could be (and were) scribes in ancient Mesopotamia, including Babylonia and Assyria, but they were a small minority and usually appear in quite specific social contexts.[1][2][3][7][9]

## Evidence for women scribes

- Texts from the early second millennium BCE show **female** scribes attached to royal palaces, such as at Mari, where ration and personnel lists mention multiple women scribes, some responsible for hundreds of administrative tablets.[1][7]
- Old Babylonian documents from places like Sippar record women scribes involved in drafting contracts, managing land, and handling correspondence for communities of consecrated women (nadītu) and other elite women.[1][7]
- Neo-Assyrian evidence includes queens and high‑status women who selected women as their personal scribes or messengers, often daughters of established scribal families.[3][9]

## Education and roles

- When women did become scribes, they appear to have received the same scribal training as men, including advanced exercises and literary copying in Sumerian.[1][8][10]
- Female literacy is also visible in letters written by merchant wives in the Old Assyrian trade network (e.g., Ashur–Kanesh); palaeographic analysis suggests that some women wrote their own letters rather than dictating to male scribes.[1][9]
- Beyond “professional” scribes, women in Mesopotamia are attested as administrators, business owners, bureaucrats, and scholars, all of which presuppose significant literacy in at least some cases.[2][9][10]

## Scale and limitations

- The scribal profession remained strongly male‑dominated: women scribes are clearly attested, but numerically rare in surviving documents.[1][3][7][9]
- Modern scholars note that women usually had access to scribal work when freed from heavy domestic obligations (e.g., celibate consecrated women, palace women, or daughters embedded in scribal lineages).[1][3][9]
- The presence of a female scribal goddess (Nisaba) and explicit lists of female scribes show that women’s participation was real, even if structurally constrained by Mesopotamian patriarchy.[7][10]

In sum: in Babylonia, Assyria, and the wider Mesopotamian world, women could indeed be scribes, and we have concrete prosopographic and documentary evidence for them, but their numbers were modest and tied to particular elite or institutional contexts.[1][2][3][7][9]
