Great question! You're right that ancient Near Eastern "beer" was quite different from what modern readers imagine—it was thicker, lower in alcohol, often drunk through straws to avoid sediment, and consumed by everyone including children as a daily staple (safer than water).

Here are a few subtle ways to clarify this without breaking the scene:

Option 1: Sensory details in the moment

When Rimmon accepts the cup, add texture/context:

*"Rimmon sat, folding his legs beneath him, and accepted a cup of beer—thick and sweet, barely more potent than the barley water his mother gave Ninqi."*

Option 2: Casual consumption cues

Show it's an everyday drink through character behavior:

*"Rimmon sat, folding his legs beneath him, and accepted a cup of beer. Ellin drank hers without ceremony, the way one might drink water, then refilled both cups from the clay pitcher."*

Option 3: Comparison to something stronger (builds world)

Reference actual intoxicating drinks to show beer isn't one:

*"Rimmon sat, folding his legs beneath him, and accepted a cup of beer—not the date wine his father saved for festival days, but the common barley drink, foamy and filling."*

Option 4: The direct approach (if Rimmon would notice)

If you want to emphasize Mukīnu's wealth, you could note the *quality* of even this everyday drink:

*"Rimmon sat, folding his legs beneath him, and accepted a cup of beer. It was finer than his mother brewed—less gritty, flavored with honey and dates—but still the same mild drink he'd had with breakfast."*

My recommendation:

**Option 1 or 4** work best for your narrative voice. Option 1 is cleanest and most subtle. Option 4 adds worldbuilding *and* characterization (Rimmon automatically assesses quality/wealth even in small things, the leatherworker's trained eye).

You could also consider whether Rimmon would even mentally call it "beer" or use your world's term. If you have an Akkadian-inspired name for it (like *šikaru*), you could use that and let context clarify, though "beer" is perfectly fine and immediately understood.

The key is making it clear through **context and casualness** that this is a common, everyday beverage—not something intoxicating or special.

“And your grandfather was married at sixteen.” Meddur wiped his hands on a rag. “Something to think about.”
Marriage. The word lodged behind Rimmon’s ribs, heavy. Before he’d built anything of his own. Before he’d decided what his own meant.
He forced his thoughts to Ellin—clever, pretty, easy to talk to. The smart path, plain as day. Yet something else flickered in his chest, a stubborn spark his brain dismissed but his heart wouldn't release. His insides twisted against it.
“I’ll think about it,” he lied.

“Go,” he said. “Your friends are waiting. But remember, you wear that cloth. Act like it.”
Friends. The word loosened yearly.
The lot behind the baker’s shop was where the boys gathered, away from adult eyes. Jugur was already there, tossing a leather ball between his hands, his shepherd’s posture loose and cocky. The twins—the milkwoman’s boys, indistinguishable in their stupidity—wrestled in the dust. The new one, a cousin from the outer oasis whose name Rimmon could never remember, sat picking his nose.
“Rim! To what do we owe the honour of your presence?” Jugur’s grin was too sharp. “Did your ghost boy  get swallowed by the vats?”
The words struck like daggers. Sometimes he wondered if Jugur knew more than he let on.
“I missed you, too, you son of a goat” Rimmon blurted, dropping to sit on a low wall.
“Catch.” Jugur tossed the ball. The mood shifted, lighter now.
Rimmon caught it, feeling the familiar weight. The same ball he’d thrown over the tannery wall four years ago, the same ball that had started everything.  “Business plans kept me busy.”
The twins laughed, identical. “Business,” one echoed. “That’s a big word for a boy who smells like his father’s shop.”
“Better than smelling like your mother’s milk,” Rimmon shot back, sharper than intended. Their faces darkened.
Jugur watched with hooded eyes. “You’ve changed, Rimmon. You used to be one of us.”
“I still am. I’m just the only one thinking past the next goat race. Someone has to figure out how to afford the goats.” He tossed the ball back, a little too hard. “Or do you plan to marry one?”
Rimmon’s barb struck true. Jugur fumbled the catch, the ball thumping against his chest. “Sheep,” he corrected, his voice tight. “I herd sheep.”
The afternoon stretched on, hot and hollow. They raced goats through the palm grove—not real races, just boys showing off, risking twisted ankles and angry fathers. Rimmon won twice, his agility serving him well, but the victories felt empty. The twins cheered. Jugur clapped his back. The new cousin looked at him with hero-worship.
It should have felt like belonging. Instead, it was a mask with a crack he could feel under his fingers.
When the sun began to sag toward the west, he made his excuses. “I have to get back. There’s a delivery to prepare.”
Jugur’s grin turned sly. “Or maybe you’re meeting someone.”
“Maybe.” Rimmon let the lie hang, let them think what they wanted. Let them believe he was sneaking off to see Ellin.
Dinner was leftover lamb with barley-cake and thyme, eaten around the low table in the courtyard. Ninqi chattered about her newest invention. Meddur listened with half an ear, his attention on the accounts. Ardni ate neatly, her eyes moving between husband and son.
“Udad is looking for a new apprentice,” she said casually. “He mentioned it at the well today.”
Jugur’s father. A shepherd.
Meddur grunted. “Udad is a fool. His sheep are sickly and his boy is lazier than a sunning lizard.”
“But well-connected.” Ardni pressed. “The spice merchant’s wife is his cousin. She was also there today, asking after Rimmon.”
Rimmon’s hand froze halfway to his mouth. The conversation snapped into focus. They weren’t talking about sheep.
“I’m not apprenticing to a shepherd.”
“You’re apprenticing to your father,” Meddur corrected without looking up. “And your father says connections matter. Tomorrow, you will go with Jugur to Mukīnu’s house. You will be polite. You will smile at his daughter Ellin. You will make her laugh. And you will remember the cloth you wear.”
The blue cloth. The mark of his trade. The promise tied around his throat like a knot he hadn’t made.
“I will,” Rimmon said.
Inside, everything braced against the word.
Later, in his room, the air felt thicker than tannery smoke. Outside the heavy shutters, the desert night stretched forever; he could hear distant night birds and a wind that rushed past the house like the beat of massive wings, making the walls feel closer.
Rimmon pulled out his knife and a fresh block of fig wood.
The candle was low, the flame barely enough to see by, but he didn’t need light. His hands knew this work. They craved it. In the workshop, his knife was a tool for his father’s legacy—precise, commanded, owned. Here, in the dark, the knife was his voice.
Usually, the wood whispered of him. Usually, when Rimmon carved, he imagined the weight of the finished piece in Andrej’s palm. He would carve a ram with crooked horns, or a desert fox with oversized ears, thinking: This curve will make him smile. This texture will make his thumb pause. It was a language of its own, simpler and more honest than any negotiation with his father.
But tonight, the wood didn’t whisper. It screamed.
Rimmon hacked at the block. No gentleness, no seduction of the grain. He wasn’t making a gift. He was making a mirror.
Shavings curled and dropped, piling around his feet like molted feathers. He worked fast, breath hissing through his teeth, chasing a shape that hurt to look at.
A bird.
He carved the body heavy, bloated with obligation. He carved the head bowed, not in rest, but submission. And then the wings. They weren't spread for flight. They were short. Stunted. Clipped close to the body, useless for anything but shivering.
A grunt escaped him as his hand slipped.
The blade bit into the meat of his thumb. Blood welled up, dark and sudden, smearing onto the pale wood of the bird’s breast.
He didn’t stop to clean it. He rubbed the blood into the grain, staining the fig wood a dull, rusted red.
My mark, he thought bitterly. Just like blue is Father’s.
He set the carving on the mat. It was ugly. Raw. When he pulled his hand away, the figure wouldn’t stand upright—the body too heavy for the crippled legs. It tipped forward, beak in the dust, perpetually falling. The blood-stained breast faced the ceiling like a dead thing begging sky.
A flightless thing. A creature made heavy by its own design.
Rimmon stared at it, his thumb throbbing in time with his heart. He wanted to crush it.
Instead he just sat in the gloom, watching the bird that couldn’t fly, while the silence of the house closed in around them both.
Settling onto his mat, back a memory of old welts and fresh strain, his thoughts slid where they always went when the house was quiet. To his own future. To hopes that had nothing to do with the workshop or family names. About what would happen if Meddur ever saw that future written on his son’s face.
After blowing out the candle, darkness dropped like a lid. Outside, the wind rushed on, wings he couldn’t borrow. Inside, he lay on his stomach, one hand curled near the fallen bird, counting his heartbeats like a man counting the bars of a cage.
—
Morning came like a sentence to be served and Rimmon rose before his father could wake him, moving through the courtyard in grey half-light. The knot at his neck felt heavier today, like a hangman’s noose. He’d slept poorly; the bird with clipped wings pecked at futures he couldn’t steer.
Meddur found him by the gate, his chestnut hair still damp from a wash, dressed in his finest linen tunic. Company clothes. “You’re early.”
“Wanted to check everything before leaving.” The words slid out smooth as oiled leather.
His father studied him, eyes narrowing. “The spice merchant’s daughter is not only beautiful, but smart. Not some goat to be won with a race.”
“I know.”
“Do you?” Meddur’s hand landed on his shoulder, heavy as a saddle. “You’ve made too many mistakes recently, Rimmon.”
The words settled into his bones like a fresh welt. Old stings on his back flared, as if his body expected the belt. He nodded, said what was expected, and slipped out before his father could look past the cloth and see what didn’t belong there.
The morning was briefly his. He checked the hides, found two with sun-rot, set them aside for haggling. His hands moved through the work automatically; his mind was trying to solve the bigger problem, turning over conversations he wasn’t ready to have, measuring risks against ledgers.
But first, this performance.
The walk to the merchant’s quarter felt less like a stroll and more like a march to the gallows. Heat rose, shimmering off baked clay walls, but Rimmon didn’t sweat. He couldn’t. The blue tunic was stiff with starch, a shell holding him upright even as his insides coiled with dread.
He met Jugur at the appointed corner. The shepherd’s son leaned against a wall, tossing a date stone, his posture loose and infuriatingly free.
“Ready to go charm her into submission?” Jugur grinned, pushing off the wall. “Man, I wish she wasn’t blood. You should see her tits. Wasted on a hide-hugger like you.”
"Jugur, shut it." Rimmon rolled his eyes and fell into step, spine rigid, chin high with the blue cloth visible at his throat—the mark of his trade following him around like a chain.
“Just saying,” Jugur barked a laugh, kicking a stone. “Mukīnu says she spends her days reading. Better sharpen that tongue of yours, Rim—you’ll have to work hard to make her think there’s more than wool between your ears.”
Their host’s house sat in the centre of the better quarter, a fortress of whitewashed walls and iron-hinged gates. A man awaited them just inside the threshold. He was older, with sharp, foreign features and skin the deep brown of polished teak. A heavy bronze collar, engraved with the spice merchant’s sigil, circled his neck, and the brand of Rabû Germa’s dominion—a stylized sun—was seared into his left cheekbone. Yet he stood with the unruffled authority of a general, his hands clasped loosely before a robe of fine, undyed linen.
“Master Rimmon, son of Master Meddur. Jugur, son of Udad,” he said, his voice a low, educated baritone that carried effortlessly across the vast entryway. “The Master of the house anticipates your visit. Please, follow me.”
He turned and led them across the courtyard—a space larger than Rimmon’s entire home, aggressively lush. Three date palms stood in a perfect triangle, shading a limestone statue of the goddess of the twin moons. The display of wealth was almost obscene. It smelled of spice and money.
The steward delivered them to the garden where Mukīnu, a man as round and soft as a rising loaf, greeted them with excessive volume, clapping Rimmon on the shoulder. He steered them toward the shade like herding sheep to a better pasture, dismissing the steward with a casual flick of his fingers. The older man bowed his head a precise fraction of an inch and melted back toward the house, his silent efficiency more commanding than Mukīnu’s bluster.
Ellin waited in the shade of the palms.
She was fourteen, a year younger than Rimmon, but sat on her tasselled cushion with the stillness of a queen holding court. A yellow headscarf matched the saffron in her father’s trade, edges embroidered with silver thread from the desert mines—a Luedina design, ironically, worn by a girl whose father called them savages. She observed them with dark eyes, taking in everything—the sweat on Jugur’s forehead, the stiff set of Rimmon’s shoulders—without giving anything back.
"Jugur," she said, voice cool as water. "And the leatherworker’s heir. Rimmon, isn't it? I see you managed to get that thyme home without losing your head in a daydream this time."
Rimmon felt Jugur’s sharp elbow in his ribs, but he kept his eyes on Ellin. "The thyme made it. My mother was less impressed with my speed, but she couldn't fault the quality of your father’s stock."
"Quality takes time to select," Ellin said, echoing the very excuse Rimmon had used on his mother. Her eyes glinted. She was letting him know she remembered his exact words. "Father told me to be impressed that you're here. I told him we’d already covered the basics of spice and leather at the market."
"And?" Rimmon asked, bracing himself.
"And I'm still waiting for the part where I'm impressed."
The insult landed.

Her smile widened, just barely. A small crack in the porcelain. “The price of hide changes weekly. I’d prefer conversation that isn’t about livestock.”

Rimmon sat, folding his legs beneath him, and accepted a cup of beer from another servant—thick and sweet, barely more potent than the barley water his mother gave Ninqi. Ellin watched him, not looking at a boy but inspecting a potential acquisition, searching for flaws the way Meddur searched for scar tissue in a hide.
“Your father tells my father you are solid,” Ellin said, peeling a fig with delicate, henna-stained fingers. “He says you make saddle patterns more exquisite than wall paintings. That you are... reliable.”
“I am efficient,” Rimmon corrected.
“Obsessive, is what Jugur says.” She glanced at her distant cousin, busy devouring fruit. “He says you never come out anymore. Always working.”
“The trade doesn’t build itself.”
“Neither does a life.” She popped a fig segment into her mouth. “So, Rimmon. What do you do when you aren’t being efficient?”
Rimmon’s fingers tightened around the cup. The beer had gone warm.
“I plan,” Rimmon said aloud. “I think about the future of the workshop. Bringing tanning and tooling under one roof.”
Ellin paused, hand hovering over the fruit bowl. “Ambitious. And risky. Our fathers hate risk.”
“Our fathers hate change.”
“And you do not?”
He met her eyes, letting the polite mask slip for a second, revealing the friction burning beneath. “I hate stagnation.”
She held his gaze. For a long moment, the only sound was palm leaves rustling and Jugur chewing. Then Ellin stood.
“Show me,” she said.
Jugur looked up, mouth full. “Show you what?”
“The architecture,” she said smoothly. “Father says you have an eye for structure. Come, Rimmon. Walk with me to the gate. Jugur, stay here and finish the dates. You look like you need the strength.”
“Your funeral, Rim.” Jugur shrugged, reaching for another date.
Rimmon rose and followed her away from the shaded centre, toward the far wall where the air grew heavier with the scent of damp earth and blossoms. Here, desert roses grew in a tangled, fragrant thicket, their fleshy petals a stark, vibrant pink against the whitewashed stone. They walked in silence until the sounds of the courtyard—Jugur’s chewing, the distant clatter of the kitchen—faded entirely.
Ellin stopped beside the blooms and turned to him. The performance dropped from her face like shedding skin.
“You’re not here for me.” It wasn’t a question.
“No.”
“Good.” She let out a breath, shoulders dropping an inch. “Because if you were another suitor trying to buy my father’s favour with compliments about my eyes or lips, I was going to push you into the onion pit.”
“My father wants the alliance,” Rimmon said, his voice flat. “Your father wants a ledger-reader.”
“And what do you want, leatherworker?”
Rimmon looked at the gold bracelets on her wrists. Heavy. Expensive. Manacles made of wealth. “I want to be anywhere else.”
Ellin’s gaze wandered to the roses, her expression softening for a heartbeat before turning grim. “My father’s household personnel consists of seventeen souls,” she said, nodding toward a young woman quietly watering the thicket. “That one? She keeps his accounts better than he does. But she’ll never own the numbers she balances.” Her eyes met Rimmon’s, dark and direct. “We’re all property here. Just different grades of gilt.”
She plucked a rose petal, rubbing its waxy surface between her fingers. “When my father said you are solid, he meant boring. Safe. He thinks you’ll keep me in a nice house and never ask why I read books you didn’t buy.” She stepped closer, her voice dropping to a whisper barely audible over the buzzing of a lone bee. “But you look like you’re suffocating. You keep pulling at that blue rag around your neck.”
Rimmon’s hand froze halfway to his throat. He hadn’t realized.
“We are both trapped in a performance,” she whispered, letting the petal fall. “But performances can be useful.”
“Useful how?”
“To buy time.” She smoothed her silken dress, the gesture practised and perfect. “If we play this right—if you smile at me, and I laugh at your jokes, and we let our fathers congratulate themselves on their brilliance—they’ll stop watching us so closely. You get freedom to plan your risky changes. I get mine.”
Rimmon saw the calculation in her eyes, the desperate intelligence of a strategist with no army. She didn’t want him. She wanted a shield.
“A partnership,” he said.
“A distraction,” she corrected. “For both of us.”
She held out her hand, palm down, as if expecting a tribute. Her skin was smooth, perfumed with rosewater. No dirt under her nails, no calluses, no scars—a hand that had never known the bite of lime or the sting of a belt.
Rimmon looked at her hand, then up at the balcony where the shadow of her father lingered.
“He’s still watching,” Ellin whispered, her voice a sharp command under a soft facade. “If you want that time, Rimmon, give him the show he paid for. Drop.”
The blue cloth around Rimmon’s neck felt like a lead weight, but he didn’t hesitate. He sank to one knee in the damp earth of the rose thicket. It was a gesture of surrender that felt, paradoxically, like a declaration of war.
He took her hand. Her skin was cool and dry. No spark. No jump of pulse. Just the cold, steady grip of two conspirators signing a treaty in the dirt.
He pressed his lips to her knuckles—a brief, hollow contact.
“Deal,” he murmured against her skin.
“Deal.” Ellin didn't pull away immediately. She let the moment hang long enough for the shadow on the balcony to settle into a satisfied posture. “Now, stand up and smile. You’re a natural liar, Rimmon. We’re going to get along just fine.”
As they walked back toward the courtyard, Rimmon was finally able to take a deep breath.
They toured the gleaming stores—mud-brick chambers with carefully placed vents, designed to keep moisture out and cool air circulating. Mukīnu explained the engineering with pride, and Rimmon listened, asked questions, made suggestions. He performed his part flawlessly.
His mind was already planning ahead. The performance had bought a reprieve. Time.
When they left, Ellin walked them to the gate and pressed a small pouch into Rimmon’s hand—dried apricots, honeyed. “For the road.” Quieter: “For the performance.”
He nodded. An ally, then. Not a future, but a delay.
Jugur was full of himself on the walk back, talking about the stores, the ventilation, how impressed Mukīnu had been. “See? I told you she’s a fierce thing.”
“Yeah.. you were right,” Rimmon said, voice distant.
As they walked back through the cooling streets, the blue cloth no longer felt quite so tight at his throat.
Rimmon’s hand went to his satchel, fingers finding the smooth shape of the bird he’d carved last night. The clipped wings.
Tomorrow, he’d carve another. Better this time. Wings spread, beak open, ready to fly.
He was just getting started.
