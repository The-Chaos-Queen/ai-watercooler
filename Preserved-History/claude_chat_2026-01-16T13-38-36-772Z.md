The Cell
[ANDREJ'S POV]
The cell smelled like piss and old fear, the kind that soaked into stone and never left. Iron bars, slick with harbor damp. A single high window that let in a shaft of light too thin to matter. The floor was cold enough that Andrej could feel it through his sandals.
They'd been here for hours.

Andrej paced. Three steps one way, three steps back. The cell wasn't big enough for anything more. His hands kept clenching and unclenching, the phantom memory of the soldier's grip still burning in his wrists where they'd bound him.
Eisenbund. Spy. Enemy.
The words circled in his head like vultures.
He stopped in front of Rimmon and dropped to his knees on the cold stone.
"Thank you," Andrej said quietly.
Rimmon looked up, his eyes red-rimmed and glassy. "For what? Getting us both arrested?"

Rimmon's laugh was sharp and broken. "Of course I couldn't. You're an idiot if you think I could just—" His voice cracked. He looked away, blinking hard. "You're an idiot."

Rimmon's mouth twitched—not quite a smile, but close. "I panicked. It seemed funny at the time."
"It was funny. For about three seconds. Then they threatened to arrest you for being my accomplice."

Rimmon went still. "What?"

Rimmon shoved at his chest, but he was fighting a smile. "You're an asshole."

Rimmon's eyes were wet, but he was laughing—quiet, broken, but real. "No take-backs," he agreed.

"At least we're stuck together," Rimmon murmured after a moment, leaning back into Andrej.

Outside, footsteps echoed. Keys jangled. Both of them tensed.
The door opened.

"You defended people who couldn't defend themselves, at great personal cost, for someone you love." Thalia's expression softened slightly. "That's not idiocy. That's loyalty. Misplaced in that context, perhaps, but loyalty nonetheless." She paused. "Tell me about Andrej."

"And you hate it."


"Yes." The word came out raw.


"Because you think Pontodoros will take him from you."

"How to survive in a world that thinks boys like you are decorative at best, disposable at worst. How to turn your 'flaws'—your emotion, your loyalty, your inability to shut up when injustice is staring you in the face—into *weapons.*" Her smile was dangerous now. "How to make men like Calvus regret underestimating you."

"Because my husband has no idea what to do with you, but I do. Because I've spent twenty years in this household reading ledgers and hosting dinners and being 'the merchant's wife' while *actually* running half his business through whispers and tea parties and well-placed gossip." That influence is more valuable than position. That the real power in any household is held by the people no one's watching."

Rimmon looked at her hand—steady, sure, offering something he desperately needed but didn't fully understand yet.


He took it.


"Teach me."



## Week Five: The Student


Thalia's lessons weren't like anything Rimmon had expected.


There were no scrolls, no formal instruction. Just... observation. Questions. Tests disguised as casual conversation.


"Tell me what you see," Thalia would say, watching the household move around them. And Rimmon would watch—really watch—as servants carried messages, as Kostas's business partners arrived and departed, as Thalia's daughter Iris practiced her lyre in the courtyard.


"The head steward is skimming," Rimmon said one afternoon, watching the man supervise a wine delivery. "He marks the amphora as full when they're only three-quarters. Keeps the difference for himself or sells it."


"Good. What else?"


"Kostas's son—Marcus—he's afraid of his father. He flinches when Kostas enters a room. Tries too hard to please him."


"And?"


Rimmon frowned, thinking. "And... Iris plays the lyre every afternoon at the same time. When that grain merchant's son visits. The one with the stupid hair."


Thalia laughed. "His name is Demetrios, and she's been in love with him for six months. He's too dim to notice."


"Should I—"


"No. Let her have her romance. It's harmless." Thalia sipped her wine. "But you're learning. You see the patterns now. People are creatures of habit. They repeat themselves. And repetition creates vulnerability."


Rimmon absorbed this, filing it away.


"Why does Kostas hate Calvus so much?" he asked one day.


Thalia's expression darkened. "Because fifteen years ago, they were business partners. Friends, even. They invested in a joint shipping venture—grain from the eastern provinces. But Calvus sabotaged it. Bribed the harbor master to delay their ships, spread rumors that the grain was spoiled. By the time Kostas realized what was happening, they'd lost everything. The investment, their reputation, nearly the house itself."


"Why would he do that?"


"Because Calvus wanted Kostas's share of the business, and he was willing to destroy him to get it." Thalia's voice was quiet, controlled. "My husband rebuilt from nothing. It took ten years. And he's never forgotten."


"So when I insulted Calvus at dinner—"


"You reminded Calvus that Kostas is 'losing control.' That he's weak. Which will make Calvus overconfident. Which my husband will exploit." Thalia looked at him. "You were a weapon you didn't know you were holding. Now I'm teaching you to aim."



The first real test came during a trip to the agora.


Thalia brought Rimmon along to "help carry purchases," but really it was a lesson in observation. They moved through the market—Thalia greeting merchants, examining fabrics, asking about shipments—and Rimmon watched how she operated.


She never bought at the first price. Never showed too much interest. Always had another option, another vendor, another plan. And the merchants—who probably knew exactly what she was doing—still gave her better deals than they'd give anyone else, because she made them *want* to please her.


"See that man?" Thalia murmured, nodding toward a wine merchant arguing with a customer. "What do you notice?"


Rimmon watched. "He's angry. Defensive. The customer is accusing him of watering down the wine."


"And?"


"And... he's lying. You can see it in his eyes. He keeps looking away. Touching his neck."


"Good. Now watch."


Thalia approached the merchant, all warmth and sympathy. "Is everything alright? You seem distressed."


The merchant immediately shifted his posture—less defensive, more grateful for the intervention. "Just a misunderstanding, my lady. This gentleman thinks—"


"I'm sure it's nothing," Thalia said smoothly. "Though I must say, I purchased wine from you last month, and it was *exquisite.* I'd hate to think there were any... quality issues."


The merchant paled slightly. Thalia was a regular customer. An influential one.


"Of course not, my lady. I'll refund this gentleman immediately. A misunderstanding, as I said."


Thalia smiled. "I'm so glad. I'd hate to have to find another wine merchant. They're so difficult to trust these days."


She walked away, and Rimmon followed, his mind spinning.


"You just made him confess without accusing him of anything."


"I gave him an option: fix this quietly, or lose my business and have me spread word of his dishonesty through every household in the district." Thalia glanced at him. "Information and leverage, Rimmon. You don't need to threaten people. You just need them to understand the cost of disappointing you."


Rimmon absorbed this, something clicking into place in his mind.


*This is power. Real power. Not strength or swords. Just... knowing things. And knowing how to use them.*



## Week Seven: The Daughter


Iris was sixteen, dark-haired and lovely in the way of girls who'd never had to fight for anything. She played the lyre beautifully, spoke three languages, and had absolutely no idea what to do with the fact that she was desperately, hopelessly infatuated with Rimmon.


It started innocently enough. She'd ask him to help carry something. To give his opinion on a piece of music. To sit and listen while she practiced. Rimmon, grateful for any friendly interaction in the household, obliged.


He didn't realize what was happening until Thalia pulled him aside one afternoon.


"My daughter is in love with you."


Rimmon blinked. "What? No. We're just—I'm just helping her with—"


"She's in love with you," Thalia repeated, amused. "She thinks you're tragic and poetic and beautifully wounded. She wants to save you."


Rimmon's stomach dropped. "I would never—I'm not interested in—"


"I know." Thalia waved a hand. "You're obviously besotted with your silver-eyed warrior. But Iris doesn't know that. And she's making herself vulnerable in ways that could become... complicated."


"What do I do?"


"You handle it gently. You don't humiliate her. But you make it clear—kindly, firmly—that her affection is misplaced." Thalia's expression was serious now. "This is a test, Rimmon. Can you be kind while still being clear? Can you protect someone's dignity while protecting yourself?"


Rimmon found Iris in the courtyard that evening, practicing a new piece on her lyre. She brightened when she saw him.


"Rimmon! I was hoping you'd come. Listen to this—"


"Iris." He sat down beside her, careful to keep distance between them. "Can we talk?"


Her smile faltered slightly. "Of course. What's wrong?"


"Nothing's wrong. I just—" He took a breath. "You've been very kind to me. Kinder than I deserved, honestly. And I value that. But I think—I think maybe I've given you the wrong impression."


Iris's face went very still. "What do you mean?"


"I mean that I care about you. As a friend. As someone who's made this place bearable. But—" He met her eyes, gentle but firm. "But I can't give you more than that. My heart is... elsewhere. With someone I left behind in Mergos."


Her eyes filled with tears. "Andrej."


"Yes."


"Does he know?" Her voice was small. "How much you love him?"


"I hope so." Rimmon's throat was tight. "I'm trying to survive three months without him so I can tell him again."


Iris looked down at her lyre, blinking hard. "I feel very stupid."


"Don't." Rimmon reached out, squeezed her hand once—brief, brotherly. "You're not stupid. You're sixteen and you saw someone you thought needed saving. That's not stupid. That's kind."


She laughed wetly. "I don't feel kind. I feel ridiculous."


"Join the club." Rimmon smiled. "I've felt ridiculous every day since I got here."


Iris wiped her eyes, managed a shaky smile. "Will we still be friends?"


"If you can stand it."


"I'll manage." She picked up her lyre. "Though I'm burning that poem I wrote about your eyes."


"Probably for the best."


They sat in companionable silence while Iris played, and Rimmon felt something settle in his chest. He'd handled it. Not perfectly, but... kindly. Firmly. The way Thalia had taught him.


*Maybe I'm learning after all.*



## Week Nine: The Weapon


"Calvus is hosting a symposium," Kostas announced at dinner one evening. "A gathering of merchants and minor nobility. Poetry, wine, philosophical debate. Very refined." His tone made it clear what he thought of Calvus's pretensions. "He's sent an invitation."


"Will you attend?" Thalia asked, though from her expression, she already knew the answer.


"I'm considering it." Kostas looked at Rimmon. "You. You know poetry, don't you? All that useless prose you used to spout?"


Rimmon straightened. "Yes."


"Good. You're coming with me."


Rimmon's heart lurched. "To the symposium? As what?"


"As entertainment." Kostas's smile was sharp. "Calvus thinks I'm slipping. That my household is undisciplined. Let's give him exactly what he expects."



The symposium was held in Calvus's villa—a sprawling estate that made Kostas's house look modest. Marble columns. Gold-leafed frescoes. Servants in matching tunics. Everything designed to display wealth, power, taste.


Calvus himself was a broad-shouldered man with graying hair and the kind of smile that never reached his eyes. He greeted Kostas with exaggerated warmth.


"My old friend! How generous of you to attend. I know how... busy you are." His gaze flicked to Rimmon, dismissive. "And you've brought your new acquisition. How charming."


Rimmon felt his jaw tighten, but Thalia's lessons held. *Don't react. Watch. Wait.*


The symposium proceeded with the usual rituals—reclining on couches, wine mixed with water, servants bringing courses of fish and honeyed fruits. The guests discussed philosophy, trade routes, the war with the Eisenbund. Calvus held court, making pronouncements with the confidence of a man who'd never been contradicted.


Then came the poetry.


Calvus recited first—something stiff and formal about the glory of the Empire, clearly written by someone he'd paid. It was technically proficient and utterly soulless. The guests applauded politely.


"And you, Kostas?" Calvus said with false generosity. "Would you honor us with a recitation?"


"I'm afraid I lack the skill," Kostas said smoothly. "But my associate here is quite talented. Rimmon?"


All eyes turned to Rimmon.


He stood, his heart pounding, and felt the weight of Kostas's expectation, Calvus's disdain, every guest's assumption that he was about to embarrass himself.


Then he remembered: *This is a weapon. Aim it.*


He recited—not Imperial poetry, but an old Eisenbund saga. The same one Andrej might be learning with Pontodoros right now, if Pontodoros was teaching him his heritage. The tale of the bear-sons who stood against impossible odds, who chose death over submission, who sang as they bled.


His voice was clear, controlled, perfect. He'd spent years memorizing poetry, performing in the oasis when his father wasn't paying attention. This was the one thing he'd always been good at.


When he finished, the room was silent.


Then one guest began to applaud. Then another. Then the whole room.


Calvus's expression was carefully neutral, but Rimmon saw the flash of irritation in his eyes. He'd expected a disaster. Instead, he'd gotten beauty. Competence. Something that made Kostas look cultured, refined, magnanimous for bringing such talent.


"Remarkable," Calvus said, his voice tight. "Though I'm surprised you'd choose barbarian verse for such refined company."


"The Eisenbund have their own beauty," Rimmon said carefully, using Thalia's lessons. "To dismiss it entirely seems... narrow. Don't you think?"


It was the gentlest possible rebuke. But everyone in the room heard it.


*You're narrow-minded. Provincial. Less cultured than you pretend.*


Calvus's smile turned to ice.


And Kostas, reclining on his couch, allowed himself the smallest smile of satisfaction.


On the way home, Kostas was quiet for a long time. Then: "You aimed that perfectly."


"Thalia taught me."


"I know." Kostas glanced at him. "You're not an idiot after all. Just very young and very reckless. But if you can learn to temper the recklessness..." He paused. "You might actually be dangerous."


*Dangerous.* The same word Thalia had used.


Rimmon liked the sound of it.



## Week Eleven: The Cost


The problem with becoming good at something is that people expect you to keep doing it.


Kostas started using Rimmon more deliberately—sending him to negotiations as an observer, having him attend social gatherings to report back on conversations, asking his opinion on business rivals. Not as a joke anymore. As an actual asset.


And Rimmon discovered something terrifying: he was good at it. *Genuinely* good.


He could read people the way he used to read poetry—seeing the rhythm beneath the words, the pattern beneath the performance. He knew when someone was lying, when they were desperate, when they were bluffing. And he knew how to use that information to Kostas's advantage.


But it came at a cost.


There was a minor merchant—a kind man named Stephanos who traded in olive oil—who was struggling. His ships had been delayed, his creditors were circling. He came to Kostas asking for a loan extension. Just a few more weeks to get his shipment in and he could pay everything he owed.


Kostas asked Rimmon's opinion.


Rimmon had watched Stephanos during the negotiation. Had seen the desperation in his eyes, the tremor in his hands. The man was telling the truth—he just needed time.


But Rimmon had also overheard (through Thalia's network) that Calvus was one of Stephanos's creditors. If Stephanos defaulted, Calvus would seize his ships. His routes. Everything.


And if Kostas refused the extension, Stephanos would default.


Which would hurt Calvus.


Rimmon looked at Stephanos—this kind man who'd never done anything wrong except have bad luck with the weather—and felt something twist in his gut.


Then he looked at Kostas.


"Refuse the extension," Rimmon said quietly.


Kostas's expression didn't change, but Rimmon saw the approval in his eyes.


Stephanos's face crumpled. "Please. I just need—"


"The answer is no," Kostas said. "I'm sorry. Business is business."


Stephanos left, and Rimmon felt sick.


That night, he lay in his tiny room and thought about what he'd just done. He'd destroyed a man's livelihood—not because the man deserved it, but because it served Kostas's larger goal of hurting Calvus.


*Collateral damage,* he thought. *That's what Stephanos is. Just a piece on a board.*


He thought about Andrej. About what Andrej would say if he knew. About whether Andrej would even recognize him anymore.


*I'm becoming someone else,* Rimmon realized. *Someone who can make those calculations. Who can hurt innocent people for strategy.*


He tried to feel guilty about it.


He did feel guilty.


But he also felt... powerful. In control. Like for the first time in his life, he wasn't just reacting to crises—he was *creating* them.


And he didn't know if that was growth or corruption.



## Week Twelve: The Realization


The last week before Andrej's return, Rimmon stood in Kostas's office reviewing a contract.


He was dressed better now—not silk, but good wool. His hair was styled. His hands were steady. He'd gained back the weight he'd lost and then some, his frame filling out with regular meals and less constant terror.


He looked like he belonged here.


Kostas entered, glanced at the contract, nodded approvingly. "Good. Send that to the Ostrium contact."


"Already done."


"And the information about Calvus's grain shipment?"


"His ship docks in three days. I've arranged for the harbor inspector to find 'irregularities' in his cargo manifest. Should delay him at least a week."


Kostas smiled. "Excellent." He poured wine, passed a cup to Rimmon. "You've exceeded my expectations. When Pontodoros first suggested you, I thought he was being charitable. But you've proven useful. Very useful."


Rimmon accepted the cup, feeling the weight of the compliment.


"Thank you."


"I'm considering extending your contract," Kostas continued. "When your three months are up. You have potential. And Thalia speaks highly of you."


Rimmon's chest tightened. "I—I appreciate that. But I made a commitment. To Andrej. When he returns—"


"Ah yes. The warrior boy." Kostas's expression was unreadable. "Tell me, Rimmon. If he comes back changed—if Pontodoros has made him into something excellent, something that doesn't fit with who you were in the oasis—what then?"


The question hit like a blade.


"I don't know," Rimmon admitted.


"Think about it." Kostas returned to his work. "You have a place here, if you want it. And places like this don't come often for boys with your... background."


Rimmon walked back to his room in a daze.


He stood in front of the small mirror Thalia had given him and looked at his reflection.


He barely recognized himself.


The boy who'd stood on the docks three months ago—wine-stained, desperate, clinging to Andrej like a lifeline—was gone. In his place was someone sharper. More controlled. Someone who knew how to navigate power, how to weaponize information, how to smile while calculating exactly how to destroy you.


Someone his father might have been proud of, if his father had ever wanted him to succeed.


*I'm good at this,* Rimmon thought. *I actually enjoy it. The chess game, the strategy, the wins.*


But then he thought about Andrej.


About whether Andrej would still want him when they met again. Whether Andrej would look at this new version of Rimmon—polished, political, capable of casual cruelty—and see a stranger.


Whether Rimmon would look at whatever Andrej had become and feel the same.


Three months ago, they'd made a pact: *We don't ask. We survive. We come back.*


But what if the people who came back weren't the same as the ones who left?


What if they'd both survived by becoming someone the other couldn't love?


Rimmon sat on his bed and waited for Andrej's return, feeling like he was waiting for a stranger he used to know.

so, right—three months are up. Next beats, in rough order, no fluff: 1. The first week back • Andrej moves into Pontodoros's guest wing. Rimmon keeps his room in Kostas's house—awkward geography, forced visits. • They settle into partial intimacy: stolen pantry blow-jobs, corridor grinds, Andrej insisting it's not breaking the rule if I don't— then finishing Rimmon anyway. Guilt binge every time. • Iris starts tailing Rimmon like a puppy. Every time he's late from business, she beams: You and Andrej look tired. Training together? Thalia smirks, refills everyone's wine. 2. Week two: cracks widen • Pontodoros tests Andrej in public—sparring demos, debate circles. Andrej wins, but flinches every time Pontodoros claps his shoulder. Rimmon watches from the crowd, throat tight. • Rimmon tries jealousy: brings a harmless Luedinan courier to dinner, flirts outrageously. Andrej's chopsticks snap. Later, in the garden, Rimmon whispers, See? Easy to break. Andrej kisses him silent, then pulls away: Not yet. • Iris finally corners Andrej alone—Teach me spear forms?—while Rimmon hides behind a column, dying. 3. Week four: the beard grows • First scruff appears. Pontodoros nods: The role nears end. Andrej stares at himself, terrified it'll make him old enough for full armor—and full duty away from Rimmon. • Rimmon climbs through his window that night, climbs into bed fully clothed. I need you. All of you. They go further—hands inside, breath caught—but Andrej stops at the belt. Rimmon leaves furious, ends up drunk in Thalia's study. She pours him tea, says nothing, just pats his head like a cat. 4. Week six: the ultimatum • Council banquet. Andrej in half-armor, shield on back—citizen now, basically. Rimmon in silk-trimmed wool, Kostas's rising star. • They dance once, formal steps. Whispered fight under the music: Rimmon—One word from Pontodoros and you sail east? Andrej—One word from Kostas and you take Calvus's seat. Both say maybe at the same time. Horror. • Rimmon drags him to the boathouse. Full clothes off this time. Andrej nearly gives in—then Pontodoros's voice echoes in his skull: discipline, boy. He rolls off, spends the night alone on the pier. Rimmon cries into his pillow, first time since the docks. 5. The breaking point • Andrej assigned first solo patrol—three weeks at the border. Rimmon gets wind, storms Pontodoros's villa. Doesn't beg; just stands there, eyes wet, says He's not ready. • Pontodoros sees it. Agrees to shorten the patrol—if Andrej finishes the final rite tonight: solo vigil on the acropolis, no food, no contact. Rimmon begs to go with him. Pontodoros: That's the point. • They stand at the temple steps at dusk. Andrej kisses Rimmon once—deep, final—then walks up alone. Rimmon waits below all night, carving initials into a pillar until his knife snaps. 6. Reunion 2.0 • Dawn. Andrej comes down, beard darker, eyes older. He doesn't speak—just pulls Rimmon behind a column and finally, fully, breaks every last chain. No arguments left. • Rimmon's first coherent thought: took you long enough. • They emerge disheveled, share one look that says we survived again. • Thalia meets them at breakfast: You both look like you wrestled a god. Sit. I'll make honey cakes. Iris: Can I learn spear forms now? After that? They're citizens, lovers, and—inevitably—on opposite sides of every council vote. Same dance, bigger stakes.
