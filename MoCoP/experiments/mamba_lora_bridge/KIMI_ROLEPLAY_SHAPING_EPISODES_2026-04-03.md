    The teahouse was a narrow room with thick walls and no windows, lit by oil lamps that turned everything the color of old honey. The owner — a silent Luedina woman with grey braids and the posture of someone who had outlived her interest in other people's problems — brought mint tea without being asked and disappeared behind a curtain of wooden beads.
    
    They sat across from each other at a low table. The tea steamed between them. Rimmon wrapped both hands around his cup because they were still shaking and the warmth gave them something to do, something to hold that wouldn't notice if he held too hard.
    
    Andrej sat the way soldiers sit — back straight, weight forward, hands on the table where they could be seen. He looked comfortable in the posture the way a man looks comfortable in a cell he's lived in long enough to stop noticing the walls, the way he looks comfortable in his own skin because he's learned to feel nothing when he touches it.
    
    The silence lasted four breaths. Rimmon counted, then wished he hadn't, wished he didn't know the number, wished he could un-know the weight of each exhale hanging in the honey-colored air.
    
    Andrej spoke first.
    
    "How is Ardni?"
    
    Of everything Rimmon had braced for, small talk about his mother was not on the list.
    
    "She's well. She runs the accounts at the Silver Scales. Half the bankers in Karzem are terrified of her."
    
    "Good. She was always sharper than Meddur deserved." Andrej sipped his tea. Steady hands. "And Ninqi?"
    
    "Married. A weaver named Tarem. Good man. They have a daughter — eight months old." Rimmon paused, felt the pause fill with everything he wasn't saying about family, about continuity, about the way Ninqi's daughter had looked at him last week with the blank, assessing stare of new life that didn't yet know to be disappointed. "Strong lungs."
    
    "Does the daughter have Ninqi's stare? The one that used to pin me to the wall when I came to dinner?"
    
    "Worse. Ardni's stare and Ninqi's volume."
    
    Something warm crossed Andrej's face, there and gone, too quick to catch, leaving only the afterimage of what might have been longing or might have been pain—Rimmon couldn't tell anymore, had lost the map of these particular territories, the old intimacy of reading Andrej's expressions before Andrej knew them himself.
    
    He drank. Set the cup down. His thumb traced the rim—the old gesture, so familiar Rimmon almost reached across the table, almost closed his own hand over that moving thumb to stop it, to hold it, to say *I remember, I remember everything, stop tracing the rim and trace my palm instead, trace the lines you once claimed you could read like prophecy.* Andrej testing whether the world was real, the way he'd done in the workshop, in the harbor room, at Pontodoros's table, always touching edges when the center felt too dangerous to approach.
    
    "And business," Andrej said. "Thalia mentioned you write for the Magistrate."
    
    "Census work. Water rights. Birth and death records."
    
    "Steady."
    
    "Steady." Rimmon held the echo for a beat, felt it vibrate in his chest, then turned it because turning it was safer than letting it settle. "And you? Where are you staying?"
    
    "The caravanserai. For now."
    
    *For now.* Rimmon felt the words land in his stomach, heavy and cold as bad meat.
    
    "The rooms on the upper floor are decent," Rimmon offered, hearing his own voice from far away, polite, hostly, absurd. "The ground floor smells like camel."
    
    "I'm on the ground floor."
    
    "Of course you are."
    
    A ghost of a smile. "I've slept in worse."
    
    "That I believe." Rimmon tilted his head, felt the movement pull at muscles too tight, too long held in one position. "How long is the leave?"
    
    The thumb on the cup rim paused. A fraction. Then resumed, the motion slightly faster, slightly less controlled.
    
    "Open-ended," Andrej said.
    
    "Open-ended," Rimmon repeated. He let the words sit in the honey-colored air, let them expand to fill the space between them. In twelve years of knowing this man, Andrej had never once operated without a structure. A schedule. A next step. The tannery had its rhythms. The gymnasium had its drills. The legion had its rotations. Open-ended was not a word that belonged in Andrej's mouth. It sat there like a garment borrowed from a stranger—technically covering everything, fitting nothing, leaving the throat exposed and the wrists too loose.
    
    "That's generous of the legion," Rimmon said carefully, tasting each word before he spoke it, testing for weight.
    
    "The legion and I have come to an understanding."
    
    "What kind of understanding?"
    
    "The kind where I stop being useful and they stop pretending I'm indispensable."
    
    It was delivered lightly. A quip. The new Andrej voice—sharp, self-deprecating, armored in irony so thick it rang when struck. But Rimmon had spent three years under Thalia learning to hear the strain beneath the wit, the way you hear a rope pulled taut before it snaps, the particular vibration of fibers giving way, and what he heard now was a man speaking from the far end of that rope, looking back at the anchor he'd cut loose.
    
    He didn't push. Not yet. He let Andrej steer back because letting him steer was its own kind of holding, its own way of keeping contact without demanding.
    
    "Are you well?" Andrej asked. "Genuinely."
    
    "Yes. I am."
    
    "You look it." The silver eyes tracked over him—not assessing, not cataloging, but touching, the gaze physical, intimate, the look of a man who had once known every scar and freckle and was now searching for what remained recognizable. "You look like someone who eats and sleeps and doesn't have to check the exits when he enters a room."
    
    "Do you?" Rimmon asked. "Check the exits."
    
    Andrej's gaze flicked to the bead curtain, then to the wall behind Rimmon where a shuttered window would be if the room had windows, the movement too quick to be voluntary, too practiced to be conscious.
    
    "Habit," he said.
    
    "That wasn't an answer."
    
    "It was. Just not the one you wanted."
    
    Rimmon let it pass. He sipped his tea—lukewarm already, heading toward cold, the mint turning bitter on his tongue, the taste of conversations that went nowhere and arrived everywhere too late. "You said you came to check on people. Your word. *Check.* Ardni, Ninqi, me."
    
    "Yes."
    
    "That's a long ride for a census."
    
    Andrej looked at him. The directness of it was still disorienting—no downcast eyes, no careful approach, just a flat, clear gaze that held nothing back and revealed nothing, the gaze of a man who had learned that looking openly was its own defense, that transparency could be a wall if you built it thick enough.
    
    "It is," Andrej agreed. "But I had the time."
    
    "Because of the open-ended leave."
    
    "Because of the open-ended leave."
    
    They were circling the way they always had—Andrej silent and stubborn as stone, Rimmon talking at edges until something cracked. Except the perimeter used to be silence and stoicism, a fortress of quiet that Rimmon had learned to breach with words, with patience, with the sheer exhausting persistence of wanting. Now it was charm and deflection, wit and the quick, self-deprecating smile, and Rimmon wasn't sure which was harder to breach, which left him more stranded outside the walls.
    
    "Are you settled?" Andrej asked, reclaiming the initiative with the ease of a man who had never stopped being strategic, only changed his tactics. "Here. In Karzem. Is this where you stay?"
    
    "I think so. My family is here. My work. I've built something that works."
    
    Andrej nodded. Whatever that cost him, it didn't show in his face, which had become very still, very controlled, the face of a man who had learned to feel things in places where no one could see them. Or it showed in a place Rimmon couldn't reach yet—beneath the beard, behind the eyes, in the hands that stayed perfectly, deliberately still on the table, the thumbs no longer tracing, no longer testing, simply present and waiting.
    
    "Good," Andrej said.
    
    "And you?" Rimmon pressed, because pressing was all he knew, because stopping meant admitting the conversation had depths he wasn't brave enough to sound. "Where is your *something that works*?"
    
    The question landed. He watched it land—not a flinch, Andrej didn't flinch anymore, but something in the set of his jaw, the angle of his shoulders, the way a man stands when the ground he expected turns out to be water, when the step he planned finds no purchase.
    
    "I'm between structures," Andrej said.
    
    "That's a diplomatic way of saying nowhere."
    
    "It's an accurate way of saying I haven't decided yet."
    
    "You always decide. You decide faster than anyone I've ever known. You decided to cross the salt flats in a night. You decided to fight in the arena ten minutes after seeing it. You decided to—" Rimmon stopped himself, the words too hot, too close, *you decided to put on the helmet and swear the oath sixty heartbeats after I didn't walk through the archway, you decided to become something I couldn't follow and then you decided to survive it without me.* "You're a man who decides. So the fact that you haven't is—"
    
    "What?" Andrej's voice was quiet. Not dangerous, exactly. But close to the border, close enough to see the customs house, the guards, the questions he wouldn't answer.
    
    "Concerning."
    
    The word hung. Andrej looked at his tea. The thumb had stopped moving, pressed flat against the ceramic, white at the tip from pressure.
    
    "I appreciate the concern," he said, and the tone was perfectly calibrated—warm enough to acknowledge, cool enough to close the door, a centurion's dismissal dressed in a friend's courtesy, the kind of voice that had ended a hundred conversations in a hundred tents across a hundred miles of campaign.
    
    Rimmon ignored it. "When did you last sleep through a night?"
    
    "Rimmon—"
    
    "Because you look like you haven't. Not recently. Your eyes are—I used to be able to read your exhaustion from across the workshop, and right now you're running on something that isn't rest. You're sharp and you're funny and you're asking all the right questions about my life, and you haven't said a single thing about yours that wasn't deflection."
    
    Andrej sat very still. The lamplight moved on his face, catching the scar at his temple, the hard new line of his jaw, the places where the years had cut and the sun had sealed, and Rimmon saw him—really saw him—for the first time since the market, saw the cost of the posture, the price of the wit, the exhaustion held in the corners of the mouth and the tension in the hands that wanted to tremble and didn't dare.
    
    "I sleep fine," Andrej said.
    
    "You're a terrible liar. You've always been a terrible liar. You just used to be quiet about the things you couldn't say, and now you talk around them instead, and I'm not sure which is worse."
    
    Something flickered. Behind the mask, behind the composure, behind the centurion and the cynic and the man who checked exits—something old and unarmored looked out at Rimmon for a half-breath, a full breath, long enough to recognize, long enough to remember the weight of being seen. The same thing he'd seen in the market. The boy in the tannery yard who'd held a wooden snake so tight the scales bit his palm, who'd looked at Rimmon with silver eyes and asked, without asking, whether it was safe to want things, whether wanting was allowed, whether he was allowed.
    
    Then Andrej did something Rimmon did not expect, could not have expected, had not prepared for in any of the thousand scenarios he'd constructed in darkness.
    
    He reached across the table and took Rimmon's hand.
    
    Not a grab. Not the desperate, crushing grip of the alley in Mergos, the night before the archway, when Andrej had held on as if Rimmon were the fixed point around which the world rotated, as if letting go meant flying apart into pieces. Just his hand closing over Rimmon's fingers—firm, deliberate, the way you'd grip a rope in a current, the way you'd hold on to something solid when the deck tilts and the horizon disappears. His palm was rough. Warmer than the tea. The calluses were different from the ones Rimmon remembered—harder, drier, mapped by years of leather and iron and cold, but the shape of the hand was the same, the length of the fingers, the particular pressure of the thumb against his knuckles.
    
    Andrej didn't say anything. He just held on, and Rimmon stopped breathing, stopped counting, stopped being a man who observed his own experiences and became simply the experience itself—the warmth, the pressure, the faint tremor he felt in Andrej's grip, the only tremor, the only crack in the entire performance, the only evidence that this was not a gesture of strategy or seduction or even affection in any simple sense. It was a reflex. The involuntary reach of a man who had been treading water for longer than he could sustain and whose hand had found something solid without consulting his pride first, without calculating the cost, without weighing what it might break.
    
    Rimmon didn't know how long it lasted. Later, he would try to remember and find only the warmth, the pressure, the moment of perfect silence when nothing needed to be said because everything was being said in the language of skin against skin, the oldest language, the one they'd invented together in a harbor room that smelled of tar and salt and the particular desperation of boys who thought they were stealing time.
    
    Then Andrej let go. Withdrew his hand to his side of the table. Picked up his tea. Drank it as though nothing had happened, as though his fingers hadn't just told Rimmon everything his mouth refused to say, as though the tremor hadn't been there, as though the rope hadn't been slippery, as though the current wasn't still pulling.
    
    "You're right," Andrej said, his voice steady again, controlled, the tremor gone, hidden, buried under whatever let a soldier sit in a teahouse and discuss his exhaustion as though it were a schedule, a logistics problem, a matter of supply lines and troop rotations. "I don't sleep well. The campaign was long. I'm tired in ways that don't have a schedule for repair." He set the cup down. "But I didn't come here to unload that on you. You have a life. I can see it. I'm not here to put cracks in it."
    
    *You already have*, Rimmon thought. *You put a crack in it the moment you leaned against that wall in the market and I saw your eyes, the moment you walked toward me, the moment you took my hand and I remembered what solid felt like.*
    
    "You said you're not here to pull the thread," Rimmon said, and his voice was steady, which surprised him, which meant some part of him was still steering while the rest reeled. "But you're here. In my city. At my teahouse. Asking about my family with the voice of a man who has no family of his own to go back to. And you just reached for my hand like—"
    
    He stopped. Swallowed. The tea was cold now, undrinkable, and he set it down because his hands were shaking again, because the warmth of Andrej's palm was fading and he couldn't hold it, couldn't keep it, couldn't do anything with it except feel it go.
    
    "Like what?" Andrej asked. Very quiet.
    
    "Like you used to. When things were bad and you didn't have the words."
    
    Andrej's jaw worked. The muscle tightened, released, tightened again, and Rimmon watched it, fascinated, helpless, the old intimacy of knowing exactly what that movement meant, exactly what it cost. He looked at the wall. At the lamps. At anything that wasn't Rimmon's face, because looking at Rimmon's face was suddenly too dangerous, too possible, too much like stepping off the edge of something high.
    
    "It was a reflex," he said.
    
    "I know."
    
    "It won't happen again."
    
    "I know that too." Rimmon paused, felt the pause fill with everything he wanted and couldn't have, everything he'd built and couldn't keep, everything that was about to change whether he chose it or not. "I wish you wouldn't promise that."
    
    The silence after this one was different. Not the careful, negotiated silence of two men feeling out each other's boundaries, testing for soft spots and retreat routes. Something rawer. Something that belonged to a rooftop in Kharash, to a tent on a bluff, to every moment they'd ever stood at the edge of the thing between them and decided whether to step forward or back, whether the fall would kill them or whether the fall was the only way to fly.
    
    Rimmon broke it. Because Rimmon always broke silences, because silence was where the truth lived and the truth was a country he couldn't afford to visit for long, because breaking things was the only way he knew to build them differently.
    
    "Stay for dinner," he said.
    
    Andrej looked at him.
    
    "There's someone I want you to meet," Rimmon continued, and his voice was steady even though his hand—the one Andrej had held—was still warm, still tingling, still carrying the phantom print of fingers that had gripped a rope in a current and found it held, still wanting to reach across the table and take back what had been taken away. "His name is Marcus."
    
    Andrej heard him. Rimmon watched it happen—no flinch, no crack, but something in the set of his jaw, the angle of his shoulders, the way a man stands when the ground he expected turns out to be water, when the step he planned finds no purchase and he has to decide whether to swim or sink.
    
    "Marcus," Andrej said.
    
    "He's a captain. Garrison detail." Rimmon kept his voice level. Factual. Giving Andrej the same courtesy of directness that Andrej had given him all afternoon, the honesty that was its own kind of violence. "He's important to me."
    
    "How long?"
    
    "A year."
    
    "Does he know about—" A gesture. Vague. Encompassing everything and nothing, the harbor room and the rooftop and the tent and the hand that had just reached across a table in a honey-colored room.
    
    "He knows I had a life before him. He doesn't know the details."
    
    "Will he mind? My being here."
    
    "No," Rimmon said, and meant it, and felt the meaning of it land somewhere painful, somewhere that understood exactly what kind of man Marcus was and exactly what that deserved. "Marcus doesn't mind things. It's one of his more alarming qualities."
    
    Andrej almost smiled. The real one—the crooked one, hiding in the beard, the one that had once been rare and precious and was now hidden under scar tissue and soldier's discipline. "A man who doesn't mind things. That must be restful."
    
    "It is."
    
    "Good."
    
    There it was again. *Good.* The word that meant *I'm glad you're safe* and *I'm breaking* and *I'll carry this quietly* and *I want to hate him but I can't because he gives you what I couldn't* all at once, layered so deep Rimmon could have spent hours excavating and never reached the bottom. He heard every layer of it and had to look away because the alternative was reaching across the table and taking back the hand that had taken his, the alternative was standing up and walking out and never stopping, the alternative was too many things and none of them possible.
    
    "More tea?" Rimmon asked.
    
    "God, no. That was terrible."
    
    "The worst in Karzem."
    
    "Then why do you come here?"
    
    "Because the owner doesn't eavesdrop."
    
    The laugh was short, rough, surprised—the same bark from the market, too loud, too real, a sound that didn't know how to be small because the man making it had been large for too long in places where large meant alive, where taking up space was the only way to claim it, where silence was death and noise was armor.
    
    They left coins on the table—too much, both of them, the old habit of boys who had never known when the next meal was coming and compensated by overpaying for the current one, the superstition of generosity as protection against starvation.
    
    Outside, the evening was dimming. Karzem shuttering its lamps for the astronomers, the city dimming itself to let the stars speak. The street was a river of fading gold, and their shadows stretched long and tangled on the pale stone, joined at the feet, separate at the heads, the geometry of proximity and distance that Rimmon had spent years learning and was now, suddenly, relearning with every step.
    
    They walked toward Rimmon's home, where Marcus was waiting. Andrej matched his stride. Rimmon felt it like a hand on his chest—that long soldier's step shortening, unconscious, automatic, the old habit surviving under everything else, under the war and the bronze and the years of not-walking-together, the muscle memory older than the oath, older than the helmet, older than everything except the first day a tall, strange boy had walked beside a shorter one and silently, without being asked, slowed down so they could stay side by side.
    
    Halfway there, Rimmon said: "You're going to like him."
    
    "Am I?"
    
    "He's uncomplicated. You'll find it confusing."
    
    "I find most things confusing these days."
    
    "That's new."
    
    "Most things are."
    
    They walked on. The stars were beginning, faint and tentative, the first scouts of the night testing the darkness before the main force. Ahead, a lamp burned in the window of a house where a good man sat on a bench with pomegranate seeds in his teeth, waiting without worry, trusting without proof, loving in the plain, unperformative way that Rimmon had spent years learning to accept and was only now, walking beside the ghost of his first and most devastating love, beginning to understand the full, staggering value of, beginning to understand exactly what he was about to risk, exactly what he was about to break, exactly why he couldn't stop walking toward it.
    
    He didn't look at Andrej. He didn't need to. He could feel him there—the heat of him, the silence of him, the careful, controlled breathing of a man holding himself together for one more street, one more door, one more introduction to a life that had no room for him and couldn't turn him away, a man walking toward his own displacement with the same steady step he brought to everything, because stopping was not an option and turning back was not a word in his vocabulary.
    
    "Andrej," Rimmon said.
    
    "Yes."
    
    "Whatever you came here for. Whatever you're not saying. You don't have to say it tonight."
    
    A pause. The stride didn't break, didn't falter, didn't change.
    
    "Thank you," Andrej said. And then, quieter, almost lost in the sound of their footsteps on stone, in the fading gold of the street, in the vast indifferent dark that was swallowing the city piece by piece: "I don't know what I came here for. That might be the most honest thing I've said all day."
    
    Rimmon nodded. That was enough. For now, for tonight, for the walk between the teahouse and the door where everything would change again, where the good man with the pomegranate seeds would look up and smile and not see what was walking beside his lover, what had already reached across a table and taken what wasn't his to take, what was still warming in Rimmon's palm like a secret, like a brand, like the only true thing in a city of geometry and shadows—that was enough.
    
    The geometry of Karzem held them both. The right angles. The clean lines. The predictable shadows. And between them, something that refused to be measured, something that had survived the salt flats and the sea and the war and the years, and was now walking, unsteadily, on legs that had forgotten how to stand on solid ground, toward a lit window and the smell of pomegranate and the terrifying, intoxicating, horrible uncertainty of what comes next.
