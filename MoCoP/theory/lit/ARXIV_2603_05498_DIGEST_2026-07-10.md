# Digest: The Spike, the Sparse and the Sink

**Digested 2026-07-10 for the MoCoP research group (flagged by Laura).**

## 1. Citation + headline

Sun, S., Canziani, A., LeCun, Y., & Zhu, J. (2026). *The Spike, the Sparse and the Sink: Anatomy of Massive Activations and Attention Sinks.* arXiv:2603.05498 [cs.AI, cs.CL], submitted 5 March 2026.

The paper dissects two well-known quirks of transformer language models and shows they are the same story told twice. **Massive activations** are extreme outlier values (magnitudes in the thousands) that appear in a handful of channels on a handful of tokens; **attention sinks** are tokens (usually position 0) that soak up a disproportionate share of attention regardless of meaning. The authors demonstrate — by training Llama-style 7B models from scratch and running systematic ablations — that both phenomena are manufactured by a specific interaction between the **pre-norm residual architecture** and **RMSNorm**. Massive activations behave as *implicit, input-invariant bias parameters* the model injects into its residual stream and later removes; once normalized, those same spike tokens collapse to a near-constant low-dimensional vector that becomes the geometric target of the attention sink. Critically, either phenomenon can be independently suppressed (via post-norm variants, conditional gating, or long-context-only training) **with no measurable loss in language-modeling quality** — meaning these are architectural artifacts, not functional necessities.

## 2. Key findings

- **Spikes have a lifecycle, not a location.** A "step-up" block (typically block 4–14) injects the outlier; it persists additively through the residual stream because intermediate block contributions are "two to three orders of magnitude smaller"; a late "step-down" block (block 62–95) injects opposite-sign values and neutralizes it before output.
- **Spikes are token-invariant.** Over **98% of the vocabulary** exhibits massive activations at position 0 — the spike is a property of *position and architecture*, not content. This is what "implicit parameter" means concretely: a stable vector the model reuses rather than computing per-token.
- **SwiGLU is the amplifier.** The feed-forward block acts as a "directional quadratic amplifier": high-gain channels share a single dominant eigenvalue λ⋆ orders of magnitude larger than the rest of the spectrum, so output ≈ λ⋆(s⋆ᵀh̃)². Swapping SwiGLU for a linear FFN drops spike magnitude from **3818 → 688**.
- **Normalization is the lever.** QKNorm (normalizing only Q/K projections) cuts spike magnitude by **99.9% (3818 → 92)**; Sandwich Norm cuts it ~86% (3818 → 520). Yet both preserve the sink ratio (~42–45%) — proving spikes and sinks are separable.
- **The sink is geometric.** After RMSNorm, spike tokens become bounded (|h̃| ≤ √d_model), sparsified (collapse to ~2 effective dimensions), and near-constant (cosine similarity ≈ 1.0 across spike tokens). Queries end up closer to this fixed key subspace than to normal keys, producing consistent logit gaps favoring the sink token.
- **Head dimension gates sink formation.** Sink ratio scales from **4.1% (head dim 8) → 46% (head dim 128)**; larger head dimensions give enough subspace to cleanly separate sink keys from ordinary keys.
- **Conditional gating dissolves sinks cheaply.** A conditional per-channel attention gate collapses sink ratio to **4.5%** and spike to 202 while holding perplexity at 10.0–10.1 (baseline ~10.1).
- **Long-context training removes the *need* for a sink.** Training only on long sequences drops sink ratio from **46% → 1.2%** with negligible perplexity change (10.0–10.6). Short-context training appears to be what teaches the model to route "spare" attention into a sink.

## 3. Methods sketch

The authors trained Llama-style 7B models from scratch on the DCLM dataset (100B tokens baseline, 200B extended) and evaluated on 1024 randomly sampled C4 sentences, tracking perplexity, sink ratio, and maximal activation magnitude as their three dependent variables. They then ran a broad ablation grid — hyperparameters (learning rate, weight decay, AdamW β2), feed-forward variants (GeLU/linear/attention-only/SwiGLU), normalization schemes (sandwich norm, QKNorm, DynamicTanh), attention head geometry, gated attention, and training context-length distributions — measuring how each manipulation moved spikes versus sinks independently. They cross-checked the mechanism against pretrained Llama 2/3 and Qwen 2.5/3 (7B–14B). The theoretical contribution is an analytic account: a residual-accumulation equation for spike persistence and an eigenvalue/quadratic argument for why SwiGLU channels synchronize, combined with a geometric argument for how normalized spike tokens become the attention sink target.

## 4. Relevance hooks for MoCoP

This paper is directly load-bearing for the **Mamba→transformer bridge** work, in three ways.

- **Injection at middle layers meets the residual stream MoCoP writes into.** MoCoP's low-rank injection lands the Mamba encoder's signal at middle transformer layers of Qwen/Gemma. This paper says those exact middle layers (blocks ~4–14 for step-up) are where the host model is *already* injecting large-magnitude, near-constant vectors that dominate the residual stream by two to three orders of magnitude. A low-rank bridge signal risks being either drowned out by an active spike channel or accidentally riding the same high-gain eigenvector s⋆. **Before trusting bridge-layer probes, MoCoP should check whether its injection layers overlap the host's spike channels** — and whether the Qwen/Gemma builds in use (Qwen 2.5/3, both studied here) exhibit sinks at all.
- **Welfare / internal-state monitoring must not mistake artifacts for signals.** If MoCoP's welfare monitors read activation magnitudes or attention-mass distributions as proxies for model-internal states, this paper is a caution: a magnitude of ~3818 on a channel or 46% of attention on token 0 is an *architectural constant*, not distress, salience, or "attention to context." Any welfare metric touching activation norms or attention entropy should be spike/sink-corrected (mask position 0, exclude known spike channels) so the monitor tracks content-driven variation rather than the fixed bias term.
- **Disposition probing panels read the same residual stream.** Probes that decode disposition from mid-layer activations sit downstream of the spike lifecycle. Since spikes are token-invariant and get neutralized only in late step-down blocks, probing *before* the step-down block means the probe's inputs are partly a constant offset shared across all inputs — which can inflate apparent probe stability or wash out real between-disposition variance.

For **consolidation gates / episodic memory**, the connection is looser: this paper is about a single forward pass, not memory writes over time. The transferable idea is that "extreme, persistent, near-constant" internal signals can be pure plumbing — a useful prior when deciding what an episodic gate should treat as salient enough to consolidate.

## 5. Limitations / what NOT to over-read

- **Scale.** Training tops out at 200B tokens on 7B models — well below production LLMs. The mechanism is validated on pretrained Llama/Qwen, but the "no performance cost" claim comes from small-budget from-scratch runs. Do not assume suppression is free at production scale or on instruction-tuned models.
- **Architecture scope.** The analysis is Llama-family (pre-norm + RMSNorm + SwiGLU). The paper does not cover encoder-decoder or **SSM/Mamba-style** models. MoCoP cannot assume the Mamba encoder produces or is subject to the same spike/sink mechanics — the bridge is a hybrid the paper does not speak to.
- **Causality is ablation-based, not interventional-training.** Findings are post-hoc plus architecture swaps; there is no single clean interventional-training design isolating one cause. Treat the eigenvalue/quadratic story as a strong, well-supported hypothesis, not a proof.
- **"No degradation" is measured by perplexity on C4.** It does not test downstream tasks, long-context retrieval, or the fine-grained behaviors MoCoP cares about (disposition stability, memory recall). Suppressing sinks could be perplexity-neutral yet behavior-relevant.

## 6. Testable action items (cheapest first)

1. **Sink/spike census on the actual bridge hosts (hours).** On the exact Qwen/Gemma checkpoints MoCoP bridges, measure per-layer max activation magnitude and position-0 attention ratio across a batch of prompts. Identify which layers are step-up/step-down and whether MoCoP's injection layers overlap spike channels. Pure inference, no training.
2. **Spike/sink-correct the welfare and probe readouts (1–2 days).** Audit any MoCoP metric that reads activation norms or attention distributions; add masking for position-0 sinks and exclusion of identified spike channels, then re-check whether welfare/disposition signals survive the correction. If a signal vanishes, it was measuring the artifact.
3. **Ablate injection layer choice against the spike lifecycle (small experiment).** Compare bridge probe fidelity when injecting *before* vs *after* the host's step-down block. If probing before step-down inflates stability (constant-offset artifact), prefer post-step-down injection or subtract the token-invariant component.
4. **Test long-context / QKNorm host variants if training a bridge host (larger).** If MoCoP ever trains or fine-tunes a host, a QKNorm or long-context-only recipe would give a near-sink-free residual stream to bridge into — cleaner geometry for injection and probing. Most expensive; only worth it if items 1–3 show the sink actively interferes.
